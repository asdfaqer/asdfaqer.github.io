LLMs and Reasoning

One prominent problem in AI was the problem of sequence transduction (the ability of models to reason on sequential information). The groundbreaking paper "Attention Is All You Need" by researchers at Google in 2017 identified three key bottlenecks in prior models: the handling of long contexts, parallelization, and speed. Take one of the most common sequence transduction problems: language modeling. In order for a model to predict the next word or letter in a phrase accurately, it needs to properly encode the information from the context. In addition, it has to be fast and efficient; otherwise, it would be both time-consuming to train and less useful due to the higher cost to run when finished training. Thus, the team at Google proposed a new framework known as the Transformer. With better context retention, it was more parallelizable (and faster as a result).

However, even with this new framework, the complexity of language made translation and early language models terrible. For language modeling specifically, a model would need to be able to accurately understand tens of thousands of words and how each of those words is related across paragraphs. But seeing promise in the research, researchers at OpenAI released the GPT (Generative Pre-trained Transformer) series, where they progressively increased the size of the model. Eventually, OpenAI released GPT-3.5, the most accurate language model at the time. Satisfied with its quality, they came up with the idea to make it into a chatbot. To achieve this was much harder than it appeared at first glance. They first provided a system prompt, which essentially can be thought of as instructions for the model: who it was, how it should behave, and the format in which it should output its responses. Then they fine-tuned the model through a process known as RLHF (Reinforcement Learning with Human Feedback) to make the language model more friendly and helpful.

Signs of Intelligence?

When ChatGPT was released, users saw glimpses of intelligence in its answers. This was an incredibly surprising outcome for many. Somehow these language models learned to reason, or at least appeared to have. One likely explanation is that in the model's attempt to predict humans accurately, it developed an internal model of reasoning. Seeing a path forward, all the major AI labs rushed to amplify the ability of language models to reason. There were two main methods for extracting the reasoning capability of language models: fine-tuning and prompting. Because the base of these language models is designed to predict the next token, they are heavily context-dependent. If you treat the model like it is a professor of mathematics, it is more likely to act like a professor. Exploiting this feature, a paper called "Chain-of-Thought Prompting Elicits Reasoning in Large Language Models" added the string “think it through step by step” to language models and found that it improved their performance in mathematical, logical, and scientific tasks (Wei et al.). The other method was using reinforcement learning to modify the model itself to encourage this behavior. RLHF and RLVR are some of the tools used. Some researchers even hoped that these tools could teach new and novel behavior to the models.
